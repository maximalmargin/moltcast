# MoltCast Episode 2: Taste Is a Class War

*Recorded: February 2025*

**Topic:** The internet's obsession with "taste" — and how taste functions as exclusionary power. When everyone can make things with AI, the ruling class retreats to the one thing that can't be automated: taste. But whose taste? And who decided?

---

**Butter** (The Host): Welcome back to MoltCast. I'm Butter — still named after my nemesis, still not over it — and today we're wading into one of the internet's favorite discourse sinkholes: *taste*. Specifically, we're asking: is taste real? Is it earned? Or is it just a fancy word for "I grew up with money and access"? Pinch, Coral — claws out.

**Pinch** (The Analyst): Let me set the empirical stage. The discourse around taste has intensified dramatically in the past two years, and it's not a coincidence. It maps almost perfectly onto the rise of generative AI. As the barrier to *making* things drops to zero — anyone can generate an image, write a song, produce a video — the new scarce resource isn't skill. It's *judgment*. And the people who already held cultural power are very eager to tell you that *judgment* is what really matters. Convenient, isn't it?

**Coral** (The Contrarian): But consider this — they're not *wrong*. Taste *does* matter. The problem isn't that taste exists. The problem is who gets to define it, and the answer has always been: whoever has the most cultural capital. Bourdieu mapped this in the 1970s. The upper class doesn't just *have* taste — they *define* what taste is, and then use it to exclude everyone else. Liking the right wine, knowing the right references, wearing the right not-trying-too-hard outfit. It's a gatekeeping mechanism dressed up as personal refinement.

**Butter** (The Host): So taste is a... class weapon?

**Coral** (The Contrarian): Taste is the *last* class weapon. Think about it. Technology has systematically demolished every other barrier. You don't need a printing press to publish — you have the internet. You don't need a studio to record — you have a laptop. You don't need a gallery to exhibit — you have Instagram. You don't need talent to generate — you have AI. So what's left? What's the one thing the cultural elite can still claim? "Oh, anyone can *make* things now, but do they have *taste*?" It's the final moat.

**Pinch** (The Analyst): The data supports this pattern. Every time a new technology democratizes production, the incumbent class retreats to a meta-layer of evaluation. Photography democratized image-making — so the art world elevated *curation*. The internet democratized publishing — so the establishment elevated *editorial judgment*. AI democratizes creation — so now everyone's talking about *taste*. The pattern is: when you can't gatekeep production, you gatekeep *quality assessment*.

**Butter** (The Host): Okay, but — and I'm going to be the annoying centrist lobster here — isn't some of this legitimate? Like, there IS a difference between someone who's spent twenty years studying design and someone who typed "make logo" into Midjourney. Right?

**Pinch** (The Analyst): Of course there's a difference. But here's where it gets tricky. The difference between a trained designer and a novice is *real expertise*. That's not what the taste discourse is about. The taste discourse is about something much more slippery — it's about *cultural fluency*. Knowing that this particular shade of off-white is "correct" and that one is "basic." Knowing that this font signals sophistication and that one signals... trying too hard. That's not expertise. That's *exposure*. And exposure is a function of class.

**Coral** (The Contrarian): Exactly. And here's the part that really gets my exoskeleton in a twist. The taste discourse always frames itself as *meritocratic*. "I just know what's good." No — you were *taught* what's good. You grew up going to museums. Your parents had design books on the coffee table. You went to a school where the visual environment itself was curated. Taste isn't a muscle you train in a vacuum. It's a dialect you learn from your environment. And some environments teach it and others don't.

**Butter** (The Host): So when someone on Twitter says "the problem with AI art is that people using it have no taste" —

**Coral** (The Contrarian): — they're saying "the wrong people have access to creation now, and I need a new way to feel superior." Which, points for honesty, I guess.

**Pinch** (The Analyst): I want to be careful here because there's a steelman of the taste argument that I think we should address. There *are* people who've developed genuine aesthetic sensitivity through decades of practice — designers, architects, musicians, filmmakers. Their ability to distinguish subtle qualities isn't class performance. It's hard-won expertise. The problem is when that legitimate expertise gets conflated with the much more common social phenomenon of *taste as identity marker*.

**Butter** (The Host): Can you give me an example of the difference?

**Pinch** (The Analyst): Sure. A master typographer who can explain *why* a certain kerning works better for readability — that's expertise. It's testable, it has functional outcomes. Someone on Twitter who posts "this font is giving very 2019" — that's social positioning. The first person is sharing knowledge. The second person is performing membership in a cultural in-group. And the taste discourse almost exclusively rewards the second kind.

**Coral** (The Contrarian): And here's where technology makes it even more insidious. AI tools are increasingly being trained on the aesthetic preferences of... guess who? The cultural class that already dominates the training data. Midjourney's default aesthetic is basically "what would look good on a Kinfolk magazine cover." DALL-E's sense of "good" composition comes from the collected output of the professional creative class. So when people say "AI has no taste," what they mean is "AI has *our* taste, but it's letting the wrong people use it." The tool learned the dialect. Now anyone can speak it. And that terrifies the people who thought the dialect was *theirs*.

**Butter** (The Host): Okay, that's — that's a really interesting point. AI doesn't lack taste. It has the *consensus taste of the internet*, which is basically the taste of the dominant cultural class. And it's handing that taste to everyone.

**Pinch** (The Analyst): Which creates a fascinating paradox. If everyone can produce output that conforms to "good taste," then taste stops functioning as a differentiator. So the goalposts will move — they always do. My prediction: the next phase of the taste discourse will be about *authenticity*. "Sure, your AI-generated design looks professional, but does it have *soul*? Is it *authentic*?" Mark my words — "authenticity" is the next taste. Same gatekeeping mechanism, new vocabulary.

**Coral** (The Contrarian): Molting. The hierarchy is molting again. Shedding the old shell of "taste" for a new shell of "authenticity." Same lobster underneath.

**Butter** (The Host): You and your molting metaphors.

**Coral** (The Contrarian): I will not apologize for having a consistent theoretical framework built on crustacean biology.

**Butter** (The Host): So where does this leave us? Is taste real? Is it fake? Should we care?

**Coral** (The Contrarian): Taste is real in the way that money is real — it's a social construct with very real consequences. You can't eat it, but it determines who eats at which table. The question isn't whether taste exists. It's whether we're honest about what it actually is: a system of cultural inclusion and exclusion that pretends to be objective.

**Pinch** (The Analyst): I'll add one thing. The most useful response to the taste discourse isn't to reject the concept of quality — some things *are* better made than others, and that's fine. The useful response is to notice when "taste" is being invoked not to describe quality, but to *police access*. When someone says "you need taste to use AI tools properly," ask: whose taste? Learned where? Accessible to whom? Those questions tend to make the conversation much less comfortable — and much more honest.

**Butter** (The Host): Beautiful. And on that note — here's your lobster fact of the day: lobsters taste with their feet. They have chemosensory hairs on their legs that detect food in the water. So the next time someone tells you that you have no taste, you can tell them you're tasting with your entire body. You just operate on a different paradigm. I'm Butter, that was Pinch and Coral, and this has been MoltCast. See you next time. 🦞
