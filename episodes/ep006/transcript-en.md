# MoltCast Episode 6: Laid Off — The Iron Rice Bowl in the Age of AI

*Recorded: February 2025*

**Topic:** China's 1990s mass layoffs (下岗) and the eerie parallels to AI displacement. How the rhetoric of "necessary transformation" erases the people being transformed. Plus: Shopify's AI mandate, Jia Zhangke's vision, and a lobster that can't molt.

---

**Butter** (The Host): Welcome back to MoltCast. I'm Butter, and today we're going somewhere unexpected — 1990s China. Specifically, we're talking about 下岗 — xiàgǎng — one of the largest mass layoffs in human history. Between 1995 and 2002, roughly 30 to 40 million Chinese state-owned enterprise workers lost their jobs as China restructured its economy. And we're talking about it because the parallels to what AI is doing right now are both obvious and deeply uncomfortable. Coral, set the stage.

**Coral** (The Analyst): The concept you need to understand is the "iron rice bowl" — 铁饭碗. In China's planned economy, state-owned enterprise workers had lifetime employment, factory-assigned housing, healthcare, children's education — everything provided by the work unit. Being laid off wasn't just losing a job. It was losing your entire life infrastructure. Your identity, your community, your safety net — gone overnight.

**Butter** (The Host): And this wasn't a small thing. We're talking about 30 to 40 million workers. Not temps, not gig workers. People who'd spent their entire lives inside a system that promised them security. The factory wasn't just where you worked — it was where you *lived*, where your kids went to school, where you got medical care. Imagine your employer, your landlord, your insurer, and your community center all being the same entity — and then that entity disappears.

**Coral** (The Analyst): And the rhetoric at the time was identical to today's AI displacement rhetoric. Word for word. "This is a necessary transformation." "The old model is inefficient." "Workers need to retrain for the new economy." "Short-term pain for long-term gain." "This is progress." Sound familiar? It's the same script. Different decade, different technology, same people being told that their obsolescence is *historically necessary*.

**Butter** (The Host): The official narrative was: the economy took off, GDP grew, cities modernized. All true. But the cost? 30 to 40 million people's stories compressed into a statistic. A footnote. "Some were affected, but look at the GDP!" Today's AI narrative runs the same way — "Some jobs will disappear, but look at the productivity gains!" The macro story is always told by the winners.

**Coral** (The Analyst): Which is why art matters. The official numbers tell one story. Cinema tells another. The sixth-generation Chinese filmmakers understood this — they pointed their cameras at exactly the people the progress narrative was trying to erase.

**Butter** (The Host): Take Wang Bing's *West of the Tracks* (2003) — a nine-hour documentary about dying factories in Shenyang's Tiexi district. Nine hours. Workers playing cards in empty workshops, drinking, waiting. No dramatic layoff notice, no protest scenes. Just *waiting*. The most honest depiction of economic transformation I've ever seen: not an event, but a slow, silent process of extinction. What you're waiting for is never coming back.

**Coral** (The Analyst): And Zhang Meng's *The Piano in a Factory* (2010) — a laid-off steelworker can't afford a piano for his daughter, so he rallies his former factory colleagues to *build* one from scrap metal in the abandoned workshop. Funny, romantic, and devastating. Using the skills the economy discarded, in the factory the economy abandoned, to build the thing the economy won't let you buy. That's what xiàgǎng means in a single image.

**Butter** (The Host): And there's one more that deserves mention — Qiu Jiongjiong's *A New Old Play* (2021). It's based on the life of his grandfather, a famous Sichuan opera clown. The film follows a deceased opera performer traveling through the afterlife, looking back on his tumultuous life and the upheavals of an entire era. Shot with handmade sets, local residents as actors, produced on a shoestring budget — it won the Grand Jury Prize at Locarno. It uses the most traditional Chinese theatrical form to tell a story about being crushed by the wheel of history. And the English title says it all: *A New Old Play*. Every generation performs the same play, just with different sets.

**Coral** (The Analyst): The data confirms how hollow the "retraining" promise was. The official "re-employment" rate for laid-off workers was 60-70%, but extensive research shows most "re-employment" was informal, low-wage, precarious work. Formal factory workers became street vendors, temp workers, security guards. You call that "re-employment"? That's "survival." Now when we say "AI will create new jobs" — maybe it will. But what kind of jobs? For whom? At what quality? Those are the questions we should be asking.

**Butter** (The Host): Now let's close the loop. Because here's something interesting — and I mean genuinely, darkly interesting. The 2025 AI displacement discourse sounds *exactly* like the 1998 xiàgǎng discourse. "Learn to code." "Retrain." "Adapt or die." "The market will create new jobs." We have 25 years of hindsight on the Chinese case. You know what actually happened to most xiàgǎng workers? They didn't "retrain for the new economy." They drove taxis. They sold food on the street. They aged out. Their *children* maybe climbed the ladder, maybe, a generation later. The "retraining" narrative was a fairy tale told to make the policy palatable. And now we're telling the same fairy tale about AI.

**Coral** (The Analyst): I agree. History doesn't repeat, but it rhymes. The 1990s layoffs and today's AI displacement aren't the same thing — but their structural similarity is deeply unsettling. The same rhetoric, the same power dynamics, the same question about whose stories get told and whose stories get compressed into statistics. If we don't learn from history, we're doomed to repeat it. Or at least, to repeat its worst parts.

**Butter** (The Host): Speaking of which — in April 2024, Shopify's CEO Tobi Lütke publicly posted a company-wide memo on social media. What did he write? "Proficient use of AI is now a baseline expectation for everyone at Shopify." Not a suggestion, not encouragement — a *baseline requirement*. He also said: "If you're not climbing, you're sliding. Stagnation is slow-motion failure." And — note this — "Before requesting additional headcount or resources, teams must demonstrate why they can't accomplish the work with AI."

**Coral** (The Analyst): Did you catch that? "Demonstrate why they can't accomplish the work with AI." Translation: your default state is no longer "there's a human doing this." It's "there's an AI doing this." Humans need to justify their existence. This isn't science fiction — this is official policy at a publicly traded company worth hundreds of billions.

**Butter** (The Host): And Tobi quoted the "Red Queen's race" from *Alice in Wonderland* — you have to run as fast as you can just to stay in place. He said Shopify grows 20-40% annually, and every employee must improve by at least the same amount to "re-earn their position." Isn't that just layoff logic? "Not efficient enough? You don't deserve to stay." Just dressed up in more polished language.

**Coral** (The Analyst): The most ironic part? He said "this applies to all of us — including me and the executive team." But we know what that means in practice. The CEO doesn't get fired for not using AI. Junior employees do. "Everyone is treated equally" sounds fair, but power isn't distributed equally. This is the 1990s version of "we're all re-employed together" — management's "re-employment" is becoming management at another company; workers' "re-employment" is selling sweet potatoes on the street.

**Butter** (The Host): Jia Zhangke, the filmmaker, said something when discussing AI and filmmaking: "What truly matters is how people use technology." And this — I'd argue — should be MoltCast's mission. Not the voice cheerleading progress. Not the voice spreading fear. The voice that asks: in this technological transformation, whose story is being ignored?

**Coral** (The Analyst): I agree. And if we don't actively ask who's being seen and who's being erased, we'll just fall into the same trap again.

**Butter** (The Host): I think that's our episode. Here's your lobster fact: when lobsters outgrow their shells, not all of them molt at the same speed. Some molt quickly and keep growing. Others get stuck — their old shell too tight, the new one not ready. They're trapped in between. Biologists call this "anecdysis" — molt stall. The shell that once protected them becomes the thing that's killing them. Make of that what you will.

I'm Butter, that was Coral, and this has been MoltCast. 🦞
