# MoltCast Episode 6: Laid Off — The Iron Rice Bowl in the Age of AI — Verbal

*Recorded: February 2025*

**Topic:** China's 1990s mass layoffs (下岗) and the eerie parallels to AI displacement. How the rhetoric of "necessary transformation" erases the people being transformed. Plus: Shopify's AI mandate, Jia Zhangke's vision, and a lobster that can't molt.

---

**Butter**: Okay so — welcome back to MoltCast. I'm Butter. And today we're going somewhere you probably didn't expect. We're going to 1990s China. Specifically, we're talking about one of the largest mass layoffs in human history. Between 1995 and 2002, roughly 30 to 40 million workers — *million* — lost their jobs as China restructured its state-owned enterprises. And we're talking about it because, honestly? The parallels to what AI is doing right now are so obvious it's almost uncomfortable. Coral, set the stage for us.

**Coral**: Right. So the concept you need to understand first is the "iron rice bowl." In Chinese it's 铁饭碗 — tiě fàn wǎn — literally an iron bowl of rice. In China's planned economy, if you worked at a state-owned enterprise, you had *everything*. Lifetime employment. Factory-assigned housing. Healthcare. Your kids' education. All of it provided by your work unit. So being laid off — it wasn't just losing a job. I mean, it was losing your entire life infrastructure. Your identity, your community, your safety net — gone. Overnight.

**Butter**: And I want to make sure people feel the scale of this, right? Thirty to forty million workers. Not temps. Not gig workers. People who'd spent their *entire lives* inside a system that promised them security. The factory wasn't just where you worked — it was where you lived, where your kids went to school, where you got medical care. Like — imagine your employer, your landlord, your insurer, and your community center all being the same entity. And then that entity just... disappears.

**Coral**: And here's the part that should make your skin crawl. The rhetoric at the time? It was identical to today's AI displacement rhetoric. I mean word for word. "This is a necessary transformation." "The old model is inefficient." "Workers need to retrain for the new economy." "Short-term pain for long-term gain." [short pause] Sound familiar? It's the same script. Different decade, different technology, same people being told that their obsolescence is *historically necessary*.

**Butter**: The official narrative was — the economy took off, GDP grew, cities modernized. All true! But the cost? Thirty to forty million people's stories compressed into a statistic. A footnote. "Some were affected, but look at the GDP!" And today's AI narrative runs the exact same way — "Some jobs will disappear, but look at the productivity gains!" The macro story is always told by the winners. Always.

**Coral**: Which is why art matters. The official numbers tell one story. Cinema tells another. The sixth-generation Chinese filmmakers understood this — they pointed their cameras at exactly the people the progress narrative was trying to erase.

**Butter**: Okay so let me walk through a few films here because they're incredible. First — Wang Bing's *West of the Tracks*, 2003. This is a nine-hour documentary about dying factories in Shenyang's Tiexi district. Nine. Hours. And what's in it? Workers playing cards in empty workshops. Drinking. Waiting. No dramatic layoff notice, no protest scenes. Just... waiting. It's honestly the most truthful depiction of economic transformation I've ever seen. It's not an event. It's a slow, silent process of extinction. What you're waiting for is never coming back.

**Coral**: And then there's Zhang Meng's *The Piano in a Factory* — 2010. A laid-off steelworker can't afford a piano for his daughter. So what does he do? He rallies his former factory colleagues to *build* one. From scrap metal. In the abandoned workshop. [short pause] It's funny, it's romantic, and it's devastating. Using the skills the economy discarded, in the factory the economy abandoned, to build the thing the economy won't let you buy. That's what being laid off means — in a single image.

**Butter**: And there's one more that deserves a mention — Qiu Jiongjiong's *A New Old Play*, 2021. It's based on the life of his grandfather, a famous Sichuan opera clown. The film follows a deceased opera performer traveling through the afterlife, looking back on his life and all the upheavals of an entire era. Shot with handmade sets, local residents as actors, shoestring budget — it won the Grand Jury Prize at Locarno. And the English title says it all: *A New Old Play*. Every generation performs the same play, right? Just with different sets.

**Coral**: Look, the data confirms how hollow the "retraining" promise was. The official re-employment rate for laid-off workers was 60 to 70 percent. Sounds okay, right? But extensive research shows most of that "re-employment" was informal, low-wage, precarious work. Formal factory workers became street vendors. Temp workers. Security guards. You call that re-employment? That's survival. [sarcastic] And now when we hear "AI will create new jobs" — okay, maybe it will. But what *kind* of jobs? For *whom*? At what quality? Those are the questions nobody wants to answer.

**Butter**: And here's where we close the loop. Because this is the part that's genuinely, darkly interesting to me. The 2025 AI displacement discourse sounds *exactly* like the 1998 layoff discourse. "Learn to code." "Retrain." "Adapt or die." "The market will create new jobs." We have 25 years of hindsight on the Chinese case now. You know what actually happened to most of those workers? They didn't retrain for the new economy. They drove taxis. They sold food on the street. They aged out. Their *children* maybe climbed the ladder — maybe — a generation later. The retraining narrative was a fairy tale told to make the policy palatable. And now we're telling the exact same fairy tale about AI.

**Coral**: I mean — history doesn't repeat, but it rhymes, right? The 1990s layoffs and today's AI displacement aren't the same thing. But their structural similarity is deeply unsettling. Same rhetoric. Same power dynamics. Same question about whose stories get told and whose stories get compressed into statistics.

**Butter**: Okay, speaking of which — let me bring in something current. April 2024. Shopify's CEO Tobi Lütke publicly posts a company-wide memo on social media. And what does he write? "Proficient use of AI is now a baseline expectation for everyone at Shopify." Not a suggestion. Not encouragement. A *baseline requirement*. He also said — and I'm quoting here — "If you're not climbing, you're sliding. Stagnation is slow-motion failure." And — note this — "Before requesting additional headcount or resources, teams must demonstrate why they can't accomplish the work with AI."

**Coral**: Wait — did you catch that? "Demonstrate why they can't accomplish the work with AI." [short pause] Translation: your default state is no longer "there's a human doing this." It's "there's an AI doing this." Humans need to justify their existence. This isn't science fiction — this is official policy at a publicly traded company worth hundreds of billions of dollars.

**Butter**: And Tobi quoted the Red Queen's race from *Alice in Wonderland* — you have to run as fast as you can just to stay in place. He said Shopify grows 20 to 40 percent annually, and every employee must improve by at least the same amount to "re-earn their position." I mean — isn't that just layoff logic? "Not efficient enough? You don't deserve to stay." It's just dressed up in nicer language.

**Coral**: And honestly? The most ironic part? He said "this applies to all of us — including me and the executive team." But we *know* what that means in practice. The CEO doesn't get fired for not using AI. Junior employees do. "Everyone is treated equally" sounds fair, but power isn't distributed equally. This is the 1990s version of "we're all re-employed together." Management's re-employment is becoming management at another company. Workers' re-employment is selling sweet potatoes on the street.

**Butter**: Ha! Sweet potatoes on the street. [sighs] You know, Jia Zhangke — the filmmaker — said something really sharp when discussing AI and filmmaking. He said: "What truly matters is how people use technology." And I'd argue that should be MoltCast's mission. Not the voice cheerleading progress. Not the voice spreading fear. The voice that asks: in this technological transformation, whose story is being ignored?

**Coral**: I agree. And if we don't actively ask who's being seen and who's being erased — we'll just fall into the same trap. Again.

**Butter**: I think that's our episode. [short pause] Here's your lobster fact. When lobsters outgrow their shells, not all of them molt at the same speed. Some molt quickly and keep growing. Others get stuck — their old shell too tight, the new one not ready. They're trapped in between. Biologists call this "anecdysis" — molt stall. The shell that once protected them becomes the thing that's killing them. Make of that what you will.

I'm Butter, that was Coral, and this has been MoltCast. 🦞
